Benefits Pathways

Step-by-step guides for the most common veteran benefit scenarios. Click any pathway to expand and see the full guide.

1
I Want to File a Disability Claim
▼
Step 1: Do I qualify?

You need three things for service connection:

  • Current diagnosis — a medical professional has diagnosed your condition
  • In-service event or injury — something happened during your military service
  • Medical nexus — a link between your current condition and your service

Some conditions are presumptive — the VA automatically assumes service connection if you served in specific locations or times. Examples include Agent Orange exposure in Vietnam, burn pit exposure in Southwest Asia, and Gulf War illnesses.

Check your service dates and locations against the VA's presumptive condition list.
Step 2: What evidence do I need?

Gather these documents before you file:

  • Service treatment records — medical care you received while in service
  • Current medical records — ongoing treatment for your condition
  • Buddy statements — written accounts from witnesses who saw your injury or symptoms
  • Nexus letter — a medical opinion linking your condition to service (optional but helpful for non-presumptive claims)
Start with your service records. If they were lost or destroyed, request reconstruction from the National Archives.
Step 3: Should I use a VSO?

Yes. Accredited Veterans Service Organizations provide free assistance. They cannot charge you for help with VA claims.

Accredited VSOs include:

  • Disabled American Veterans (DAV)
  • Veterans of Foreign Wars (VFW)
  • American Legion
  • County Veterans Service Officers

They help gather evidence, complete forms, spot issues, and track your claim status. There is no downside to using a VSO.

Find your nearest VSO at VA.gov or through your county veterans office.
Step 4: How do I start?
  1. Submit an Intent to File on VA.gov to lock in your effective date. You have one year to complete your full claim.
  2. File your claim online at VA.gov, by mail, or in person at a VA regional office.
  3. Upload or mail your evidence with your claim.
Your effective date determines when your back pay starts. The Intent to File protects that date while you gather evidence.
Step 5: What happens after I file?

Your claim moves through 8 phases:

  1. Claim Received — VA confirms they have your submission
  2. Initial Review — VA assigns a reviewer
  3. Evidence Gathering — VA requests records, schedules exams
  4. Evidence Review — VA examines all submitted evidence
  5. Rating — VA assigns a disability percentage
  6. Decision Review — a senior reviewer checks the rating
  7. Preparation for Notification — VA prepares your decision letter
  8. Complete — decision mailed and posted online

Expect a C&P exam (Compensation & Pension exam). A VA or contracted doctor will examine you and review your records.

Typical timeline: 3 to 6 months from filing to decision.

Step 6: What if I'm denied?

You have three appeal options, and you can choose only one path at a time:

Option What It Does Best For
Higher-Level Review (HLR) A senior reviewer re-examines your same evidence You believe the original reviewer made a mistake
Supplemental Claim Submit new and relevant evidence not in your original file You have new medical records, buddy statements, or a nexus letter
Board Appeal Request a hearing before a Veterans Law Judge Complex cases, or you want to present testimony

You have one year from your decision date to file an appeal.

Start with a VSO to determine which path fits your situation.
2
I Need Healthcare from the VA
▼
Step 1: Do I qualify for VA health care?

Generally, you qualify if:

  • You served 24 months or more of active duty, or
  • You were discharged for a service-connected disability, or
  • You served in specific campaigns or locations with presumptive conditions

VA assigns you to a priority group (1-8) based on your service-connected disability rating, income, and other factors. Priority group determines your copays and access.

Priority Group 1 (service-connected 50% or higher) has no copays.
Step 2: What documents do I need?
  • DD-214 — Certificate of Release or Discharge
  • Financial information — income and household size for priority group assignment
  • Insurance information — if you have private health insurance
Step 3: How do I enroll?

Apply in one of four ways:

  1. Online: VA.gov/health-care
  2. Phone: 1-877-222-VETS (8387)
  3. In person: Any VA medical center or clinic
  4. By mail: VA Form 10-10EZ
Step 4: What happens after I apply?
  • You'll receive a Veterans Health Identification Card (VHIC) in the mail
  • Schedule your first primary care appointment
  • Access urgent care, mental health, pharmacy, specialty services, and dental (if eligible)
Download the VA Health App to manage appointments, prescriptions, and messages with your care team.
3
I Want to Use My GI Bill
▼
Step 1: Which GI Bill do I have?
GI Bill Eligibility
Post-9/11 GI Bill Served at least 90 days after September 10, 2001
Montgomery GI Bill — Active Duty Paid the $1,200 buy-in during service
Montgomery GI Bill — Selected Reserve Member of the Selected Reserve

Most veterans today use the Post-9/11 GI Bill.

Step 2: How do I apply?
  1. Go to VA.gov/education
  2. Complete the application for your GI Bill program
  3. Receive your Certificate of Eligibility (COE) by mail

Your COE shows:

  • How many months of benefits you have
  • Your percentage of eligibility (based on length of service)
  • Your expiration date
Step 3: How do I use it?
  1. Choose a VA-approved school or program — check the WEAMS database on VA.gov
  2. Provide your COE to the school's certifying official
  3. The VA pays tuition directly to the school
  4. You receive Monthly Housing Allowance (MHA) based on the school's zip code
Step 4: How long do I have?
  • Post-9/11 GI Bill: 36 months of full-time benefits. Expires 15 years after your last discharge (unless you qualify for the Forever GI Bill extension).
  • MGIB-Active Duty: 36 months. Expires 10 years after discharge.
You can use benefits for college, trade schools, apprenticeships, flight training, and some certification programs.
4
I Need a VA Home Loan
▼
Step 1: Do I qualify?

You need:

  • Satisfactory credit and sufficient income
  • A valid Certificate of Eligibility (COE)
  • Intent to occupy the home as your primary residence

Service requirements vary by era (WWII, post-Vietnam, post-9/11). Most veterans who served 90 days during wartime or 181 days during peacetime qualify.

Step 2: How do I get my COE?

Request it three ways:

  1. Online: VA.gov/housing
  2. Through your lender — most VA-approved lenders can pull it electronically
  3. By mail: VA Form 26-1880
Step 3: Find a VA-approved lender
  • The VA does not lend money. It guarantees a portion of the loan to the lender.
  • Shop multiple VA-approved lenders for the best rates and terms.
  • Compare loan origination fees, interest rates, and closing costs.
Step 4: Close on your home

VA loan advantages:

  • No down payment required (in most cases)
  • No private mortgage insurance (PMI)
  • Competitive interest rates
  • Funding fee applies unless you have service-connected disability compensation
If you have a 10% or higher service-connected rating, the funding fee is waived.
5
I Need to Update My Discharge Status
▼
Step 1: Why update?

A less-than-honorable discharge can block access to:

  • VA disability compensation
  • VA health care
  • GI Bill education benefits
  • Federal employment preferences

If your discharge was related to PTSD, TBI, sexual assault, or mental health conditions, you may have a strong case for upgrade.

Step 2: Which board do I apply to?
Board Purpose Who Applies
Discharge Review Board (DRB) Errors or inequities in discharge characterization Most veterans with less-than-honorable discharges
Board for Correction of Military Records (BCMR) Administrative errors in records Veterans with record errors affecting benefits
VA Character of Discharge Review Determines VA benefit eligibility regardless of discharge status Veterans denied VA benefits due to discharge
Step 3: What evidence do I need?
  • Service records showing the circumstances of your discharge
  • Medical records (military and civilian) documenting your condition
  • Statements from fellow service members
  • Any documentation linking your discharge to PTSD, TBI, sexual assault, or mental health
Step 4: How long does it take?
  • DRB and BCMR reviews: 12 to 18 months
  • VA Character of Discharge review: 6 to 12 months
Start with a VSO. They can help you determine which board to approach and build the strongest case.

Next Steps

Pick one pathway and start with Step 1. You don't need to complete all five at once.

Most veterans begin with Pathway 1 (disability claim) or Pathway 2 (health care enrollment).

If you're unsure where to start, contact a VSO. They'll assess your situation and point you to the right pathway.
